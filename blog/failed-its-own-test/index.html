I built a site that publishes its own limits, then it failed its own test

I put this off for about two years.

Not because it was hard. It's four HTML files and a stylesheet, no build step, no framework, no JavaScript anywhere. I could have finished it in a weekend, and eventually I did. I put it off because a personal site is a public claim that you are worth reading about, and every time I opened an editor to start one, my brain would render, in high detail, the face of a specific senior engineer I met once at a conference in 2024, scrolling my landing page, and going "hm."

The anxiety was never really about being judged badly. It was about being judged accurately. Those are different fears and the second one is much worse, because there's no argument you can make against it.

Every engineering portfolio makes the same unfalsifiable claim

I read a lot of them before building this, which was a mistake in roughly the way that reading WebMD is a mistake. They all say the same thing. Built a distributed system. Scalable, production-ready, 3x faster, handles millions of requests. And every engineer who reads that discounts it on contact, because they have written those exact words themselves about something that ran on a laptop under synthetic load for ninety seconds.

The claim isn't false, usually. It's unfalsifiable, which is worse. If I tell you my query engine is 3.2x faster and stop there, you have to decide whether to trust me, and you don't know me. The number does no work. It's decoration with a decimal point in it.

So the site got built around the opposite move. Every project says what it enforces, and next to that, what it does not. The 3.2x is optimizer-on versus optimizer-off on my own engine, measured with Criterion against a synthetic TPC-H-style query at about 900k rows, and it is not a comparison to any other system. Now you can price it yourself. I have handed you the tools to discount me correctly, which is a strange thing to want, but it turns out to be the only version of the claim that survives contact with someone competent.

I would love to tell you this was pure intellectual honesty, and a real amount of it is. Some of it is that if I name the flaw first, you cannot hand it to me, and there is a version of me that has been running that play in conversations since roughly age fourteen. The epistemics and the coping mechanism happen to point the same direction here. I decided not to look too closely at the ratio.

The ledger

The pattern needed a shape, so it became a ledger. Each project gets two panels. Capability is a credit, marked +, in ultramarine. Every named limit is a debit, marked −, in brass. Double-entry, which is a slightly precious way to describe two divs, but the constraint did real work: brass appears nowhere else on the site. Not in a heading, not in a link, not as an accent. If you see brass, you are reading a limit. The color is load-bearing.

I was pleased with this for about a week.

Then someone actually read it

And told me the caveats were eating the work. Roughly half the page was capability and half was disclaimer, and the thing about a fifty-fifty ratio is that it does not read as rigor. It reads as a man apologizing.

The underlying bug was a category error. I had filed three different kinds of things under one label, and only one of them was a gap.

Scope limits. Arbor is SELECT-only. No subqueries, no HAVING, no cost model. Those are genuine gaps, they belong in the panel, they stay.

Measurement caveats. "This is a k6 benchmark under synthetic load, not production traffic." That is not a gap. That is precision about a number, and it belongs inline, next to the number, where it does its job.

Design decisions. "Deferred containment is a deliberate tradeoff, proof over speed." That is the single smartest thing in Interlock. I had filed it under known gaps, where it read as a confession.

Look at what that did to the Interlock panel specifically. Three bullets. One of them was "178 tests," which is an achievement. One was a benchmark caveat. One was a design decision I am actively proud of. Zero of them were gaps. I had built a component called known gaps and then filled it with things that were not gaps, which is precisely the failure the component exists to prevent. The tool did not catch the thing it was built to catch, on its first real input, which was me.

Zenith came out worse. Two bullets under enforces against one under known gaps, on a distributed authorization engine with recursive expansion and cycle detection. The ratio itself was making an argument I did not intend, which is that there was not much there.

A stance applied uniformly to four projects stops being a stance and becomes a verbal tic. Tics read as insecurity, not rigor.

There is a real difference between someone who tells you the limits of their work and someone who cannot stop pre-apologizing for it, and I had drifted from the first into the second without noticing. Which, if you have met me, is not a surprising place to end up.

What changed

The gaps panel now appears twice instead of four times, on Interlock and Arbor, the two projects with genuine scope boundaries. Measurement caveats moved inline, into parentheticals sitting next to the numbers they qualify. Deferred containment got promoted into Interlock's opening paragraph, where it belongs, described as the tradeoff it is rather than the weakness it isn't. Zenith and Dispatch lost the panel entirely and got more substance under enforces instead.

The ratio is now something like eighty-twenty. The stance survived because it stopped being everywhere. That seems like it should have been obvious, and it was not obvious to me for two months.

The parts that are just taste

No framework, no build step, no dependencies, no JavaScript. This is not asceticism, it's that a personal site which needs a build pipeline is a personal site you will stop updating in eight months, and I know exactly which kind of person I am about that.

The project numbers come from a CSS counter, so adding or reordering a project renumbers the section on its own: 

.block      { counter-reset: entry; }
.entry      { counter-increment: entry; }
.entry-head::before {
  content: counter(entry, decimal-leading-zero);
}

Three rules, and the register can never disagree with itself. I think about this more than is reasonable.

There is no dark mode toggle. Your operating system already knows which one you want and it told the browser, so the site just listens. I am not going to make you say it twice. The scroll reveals and the reading progress bar at the top of this post are CSS scroll-driven animations, and where the browser doesn't support them, everything is simply visible. Nothing on this page depends on a script running successfully.

The whole thing is set in IBM Plex, mono for anything that behaves like an instrument label and serif for prose like this. Hairlines everywhere. Tabular figures so the dates line up down the right edge. The page is trying to look like a register, because the argument it is making is an accounting argument.

Known gaps

It would be a poor look to write all of this and then not run the site through its own component.

Known gaps

  • This is one post. A blog with one post is a claim about future behavior, not evidence of it, and you should read it that way
  • There are no analytics on this site, deliberately, which means every claim I just made about what "reads better" is a guess I have no mechanism to check
  • This page is optimized for maybe forty people. Recruiters screen the resume and will never see it. It is built for engineers who arrive from a GitHub link or an email signature, and I built it anyway
  • The 3.2x is still the number I am least comfortable with, and I have now brought it up three times in one essay, which is its own kind of tell

The thing I did not expect is that publishing the limits made the work feel more like mine. When you strip out every claim you cannot personally defend, what is left is smaller, and it is actually yours. I keep turning that over. It seems like it should apply to considerably more than websites.

Anyway. Four HTML files and a stylesheet. Took two years and a weekend.
